How the boat turns

Port and starboard

Port is the cox's left and starboard the cox's right. The rowers face the stern, so a port rower's blade is on their right hand. Here port is red and starboard green, like a boat's navigation lights.

Seat numbers

Seats count from the bow: bow seat is 1 and stroke is 8, right in front of you. In this standard rig, even seats row on port and odd seats on starboard.

The stronger side pushes the bow away

If starboard pulls harder than port, the bow swings to port. One strong rower is enough to do it. Steering against it costs speed, so it helps to know who is pulling.

Bow seats turn the boat more

Rowers near the bow sit far from the middle of the boat, so the same pull has more leverage on the turn. That is why "bow pair, row" is the usual call for a small correction at low speed.

The rudder needs speed

Rudder grip grows with the square of speed. At a standstill it does nothing, and every time you use it the boat slows a little. Going backwards it steers the opposite way and barely grips.

Stopping

"Weigh enough" stops the rowing and the boat glides a long way. "Hold water" squares the blades in the water and stops the boat in a couple of lengths. Holding water on one side only at speed slews the boat hard, so hold both sides and ease in.

Spinning the boat

To turn around at a standstill, one side rows while the other backs it down. The boat pivots toward the side that is backing. Backing is weaker than rowing, so the boat creeps forward as it turns.

Puddles

Each blade leaves a small swirl where it comes out of the water. Even, straight rows of puddles mean the crew is rowing together.

About this model

The physics is simplified and tuned to feel right. It is not a validated simulation. Real boats also roll from side to side, get pushed by wind and stream, and have crews who don't always do what you say. In the UK you may hear "easy all" for weigh enough.
